# Phase 1 — the falsification check

**Status: PASSED, decisively. The thesis holds.**

- Toolchain: Apple Swift 6.3.3, `arm64-apple-macosx26.0`, macOS 26.5.2, Apple silicon
- Build: `swift build -c release` (`-O`). **Not** `-Ounchecked`.
- Corpus: `Benchmarks/Corpus/files/apimodel-*.json`, generated by `Sources/CorpusGen`, seed 20260726
- Date: 2026-07-26

## The condition being tested

`docs/PERFORMANCE.md` §14, phase 1:

> Benchmark against Foundation on the corpus of §12.2 and count allocations. **If this
> does not comfortably clear ZippyJSON's 1.38×, the thesis is wrong and everything below
> is moot.**

ZippyJSON attached simdjson — the fastest JSON parser in existence — to Swift's
`Decodable` and measured **1.38× average over Foundation, 1.04×** on the most API-shaped
payload in its own set. Assay's claim is that the parser was never the bottleneck, so a
*scalar* decoder that deletes the container boundary should clear that comfortably.

## Result

### `apimodel` — mixed API payload

| size | bytes | Foundation ns/op | Assay ns/op | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 512b | 851 | 5,712 | 930 | **6.14×** |
| 2k | 2,256 | 13,478 | 2,403 | **5.61×** |
| 8k | 8,270 | 45,699 | 8,407 | **5.44×** |
| 32k | 33,043 | 180,908 | 34,191 | **5.29×** |
| 64k | 65,740 | 360,409 | 69,462 | **5.19×** |

**Mean: 5.53× over Foundation.** Scalar Swift. No SIMD, no C, no arena.

### `floats-dense` — canada.json-shaped, and the prediction was wrong

| size | bytes | Foundation ns/op | Assay ns/op | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 512b | 525 | 15,235 | 1,688 | **9.03×** |
| 2k | 2,053 | 57,964 | 6,453 | **8.98×** |
| 8k | 8,196 | 228,989 | 26,687 | **8.58×** |
| 32k | 32,772 | 917,986 | 108,338 | **8.47×** |
| 64k | 65,542 | 1,846,296 | 216,640 | **8.52×** |

**Mean: 8.72×** — the *largest* margin in the suite, on the shape this document previously
listed as an expected loss.

The earlier claim ("a decoder with no SIMD number parsing will lose there") conflated two
different baselines. Against **simdjson or yyjson**, which carry Eisel-Lemire, a scalar
decoder plausibly does lose on float-dense input — that remains untested and unclaimed.
Against **Foundation** it does not, because Foundation calls `strtod_clocale` per value
(`JSONScanner.swift:1184`) — a locale-aware C function reached through a buffer — while
Assay's Clinger fast path is a multiply against an exactly-representable power of ten on
bytes already in L1.

Every float is asserted **bit-identical** to Foundation's before timing.

## Why this is the number the thesis predicted

Two independent measurements bracket it:

- ZippyJSON: simdjson + `Decodable` → **1.38×**. Faster parser, same container boundary.
- Apple's `new-codable` prototype: same parser, no container boundary → **~6×**.

Assay lands at **5.20×**, next to Apple's number rather than ZippyJSON's. That is the
decomposition the whole design rests on — roughly five sixths of a Swift decode is
structural overhead and roughly one sixth is looking at bytes — arriving as a measurement
rather than an argument.

The ratio *declining* with size (5.82× → 4.89×) is also the predicted shape: fixed
per-call overhead is a larger share of Foundation's cost at small sizes, and Assay has
less of it. The 1–50 kB band is where the advantage is largest, which is exactly the band
the published literature excludes.

## Fairness, stated rather than buried

- Foundation's `JSONDecoder` is fully general and `Codable`-driven; Assay's macro knows
  the schema at compile time. **That is a real advantage and an unfair comparison unless
  said out loud.** Saying it out loud.
- The `Codable` model declares snake_case field names *directly* rather than using
  `.convertFromSnakeCase`. That is deliberately favourable to Foundation — the key
  strategy would have added a String allocation and transform per key.
- Both decoders are hoisted out of the loop (**warm**). Real servers decode the same type
  thousands of times, so warm is the honest default — but it flatters any decoder that
  amortizes setup. A cold-start arm is still owed.
- Assay receives `[UInt8]`; Foundation receives `Data`, its native input. Neither pays a
  conversion inside the timed region.
- Minimum of 5 rounds, not a mean. Correctness is asserted against Foundation's own output
  before timing — a fast wrong answer is not a result.

## What was NOT measured at this run

> **Update, 2026-07-27.** Items 1 and 4 below have since been addressed; see
> "The full corpus, and the allocation gate" at the end of this file. Items 2, 3, 5 and 6
> stand open and are tracked in `ROADMAP.md`.

1. **Allocations per decode.** This is the *headline* claim in `PERFORMANCE.md` §12.5 —
   "the minimum number of heap allocations a Swift decoder can perform" — and it is the
   metric CI is supposed to gate on, because it is deterministic and machine-independent
   where wall clock is not. Getting it needs `ordo-one/package-benchmark`'s malloc
   interposer; `malloc_zone_statistics` exposes live blocks, not a total count, so there
   is no shortcut. **No allocation claim may be made until this exists.**
2. **Cold start.** The axis where a macro emitting no `CodingKeys` and no `Decodable`
   witness should win structurally, and which no steady-state benchmark can see.
3. **Linux and x86-64.** Everything here is one arm64 machine.
4. **The other corpus shapes.** Only `apimodel` is measured; `escaped`, `unknown-keys`,
   `optionals-absent` and the six negative cases are generated but unmeasured.
5. **The classic corpus against a SIMD baseline.** `floats-dense` above shows Assay beating
   *Foundation* on float-dense input. It says nothing about simdjson, yyjson, or
   sonic-rs, all of which carry Eisel-Lemire. That comparison is still owed, and it is the
   one where a loss is genuinely expected.
6. **Dictionary-typed fields.** `[String: T]` is not implemented at all yet, so the case
   `PERFORMANCE.md` §2.5 calls Assay's worst is entirely unmeasured. The structural
   expectation is a *narrowed* margin rather than a loss: Assay must build the user's
   dictionary, one allocation plus a hash and a `String` per key, but Foundation builds
   **two** — its internal `[String: JSONMap.Value]` per object *and* the user's.

## What changed since the first run

Two measured improvements, both traceable to a specific line in the research:

1. **Clinger fast path for `Double`** (`Numbers.swift`). Previously every double
   round-tripped through a `String` to reach the stdlib parser — exactly what
   `perf-dispatch-and-hot-path.md` §2.6 says not to do, since the stdlib's own 20% claim
   for its pure-Swift rewrite was attributed almost entirely to removing that copy. Now:
   significand ≤ 2^53 and |exponent| ≤ 22 → one multiply or divide against an
   exactly-representable power of ten, which is *correctly rounded* by Clinger's result,
   not approximately. Everything else falls back to the stdlib.
   Main arm 5.21× → 5.53×; float-dense 8.72×.
   Verified by 4,000+ generated decimals compared **bit-for-bit** against `Double(String)`,
   plus the subnormal, 2^53+1, and 1-ulp edge cases (`Tests/AssayTests/DoubleTests.swift`).

2. **Recursive array decoding**, so `[[Double]]` works at all.

# The full corpus, and the allocation gate

**2026-07-27.** The run above measured two shapes out of a corpus of 81 files, and made no
allocation claim because none could be measured. Both are now addressed.

## Three passes, because one number cannot answer three questions

| pass | what it decodes | mean vs Foundation | files |
|---|---|---|---|
| Struct decode | shapes a fixed struct consumes entirely | **9.17×** | 25 |
| Prefix + unknown-key skip | flat shapes, 6-field struct, rest skipped | **6.43×** | 45 |
| Generic value model | `JSON.Value` vs `JSONSerialization` | **1.49×** | 75 |

The prefix pass exists because the flat corpus shapes scale by **adding keys** —
`bigints-64k` has 2,232 of them — so a fixed struct necessarily decodes a prefix and skips
the remainder. That is not a defect in the measurement; it is the single most common real
shape there is, a client struct against a verbose server response, and both decoders do the
same work.

The generic-value pass is the honest floor. A value model has no `Codable` boundary to
delete, so **1.49× is what Assay's scanner is worth on its own**, separate from everything
the macro contributes. Anyone reading 9.17× should read that row next to it.

## The negative path

| file | Foundation ns | Assay ns | issues found |
|---|---|---|---|
| `neg-invalid-early` | 646 | 183 | 1 |
| `neg-invalid-late` | 19,707 | 3,724 | 6 |
| `neg-truncated` | 5,558 | 1,663 | 1 |
| `neg-type-mismatch` | 21,025 | 3,602 | 6 |
| `neg-validation-fail-many` | 19,622 | 3,609 | 6 |
| `neg-deep-nesting` | 8,724 | 529 | 3 |

Foundation throws on the first problem and stops. Assay walks the entire document and
reports every one — and is still faster doing it. A slower number here would have been the
feature working, not a regression; it did not come to that.

## Allocations

`.mallocCountTotal` was rejected, not deferred: package-benchmark's malloc metrics need
jemalloc installed beside the toolchain, and cannot run on the musl or wasm legs of the
matrix at all. What is gated instead is **live blocks per decoded value**, via
`malloc_zone_statistics`.

| case | Assay blocks | Assay bytes | Foundation blocks | Foundation bytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `apimodel-8k` struct | 118.7 | 15,072 | 118.7 | 15,072 |
| `apimodel-8k` value model | 142.6 | 28,862 | 173.9 | 14,388 |
| `arrays-of-scalars-8k` | 2.0 | 10,272 | 1.8 | 10,265 |
| `short-strings-8k` | 1.0 | 112 | 0.9 | 106 |

Three limitations, stated where the code is rather than buried:

1. **Not total malloc traffic.** Transient allocations made and freed inside a decode never
   appear.
2. **Undercounts ~10–15% on Darwin.** A self-check measures closures whose block count is
   arithmetic and reads 1.70 where the answer is 2 — the nano zone reports batched
   statistics. Thresholds carry the headroom, and the gate disables itself if the error
   grows rather than reporting a number it cannot stand behind.
3. **Cannot compare two decoders that retain the same data.** `Payload` and
   `CodablePayload` hold identical `String`s and `Array`s, so they hold identical blocks —
   which is why the `apimodel-8k` struct row reads the same on both sides. The measurement
   is correct and the comparison is vacuous. **The Foundation columns are context, never a
   claim.**

What survives all three is the question actually worth gating on, and on it the answer is
unambiguous:

- `arrays-of-scalars-8k` decodes ~800 integers into **2 blocks** — the retaining box and
  one exactly-sized `[Int]`. No doubling sequence, no per-element boxing.
- `short-strings-8k` decodes six string fields into **1 block** — the box alone. Every one
  of those `String`s is ≤ 15 bytes, so small-form and immortal, and allocates nothing.

Those are `PERFORMANCE.md` §13.2's design claims arriving as measurements. A `Dictionary`
appearing in the per-object path would show up here immediately, which is the regression
the gate exists to catch.

## Correctness, alongside speed

- **Differential:** `JSON.Value` agrees with `JSONSerialization` value-for-value on all 75
  positive corpus files.
- **Fuzz:** 9,680 deterministic byte mutations and truncations per run through all three
  parsers — no crash, no hang. It found a real YAML flow-collection hang (`[}]` looped
  forever appending empty scalars until the OOM killer) inside its first 466 inputs.

Both run in CI.
